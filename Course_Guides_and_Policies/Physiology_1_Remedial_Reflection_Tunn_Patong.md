The pre-class preparation covered three main physiological topics: gastrointestinal hormones, gastrointestinal secretions, and the control of food intake. In the hormone module, the professor emphasized that remembering the basic function of each hormone is the most useful foundation for clinical reasoning. Specifically, gastrin stimulates gastric acid secretion and mucosal growth, cholecystokinin (CCK) triggers gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme release while slowing gastric emptying, and secretin stimulates pancreatic ducts to release bicarbonate-rich fluid to neutralize stomach acid. Meanwhile, vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) induces smooth muscle relaxation and stimulates intestinal water and electrolyte secretion, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) acts as an incretin to boost glucose-dependent insulin release and reduce appetite, and somatostatin serves as the universal inhibitor that suppresses the release and action of other gastrointestinal hormones. The secretion module covered the mechanisms of salivary, gastric, pancreatic, and biliary secretions, focusing on how histamine, gastrin, and acetylcholine work together to activate the parietal cell proton pump, balanced by protective prostaglandins and bicarbonate. Finally, the food intake module explained how the hypothalamus regulates hunger and satiety through signals like ghrelin and leptin, balancing hunger-promoting neuropeptide Y and agouti-related peptide neurons against satiety-promoting pro-opiomelanocortin and alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone pathways.

After missing the morning session, I met up with my friends to go over the eight clinical cases they worked through in class. In the first case, a patient with duodenal ulcers on a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) had a very high serum gastrin level. My friends explained that we need to determine whether the high gastrin is a normal physiologic reaction to the PPI blocking stomach acid, or if it is caused by a gastrin-producing tumor. To tell them apart, doctors check gastric pH and use the secretin stimulation test, which abnormally spikes gastrin only in patients with a gastrinoma. The second case involved a patient with severe watery diarrhea and low potassium from a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor secreting VIP. My classmates explained that giving octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, mimics somatostatin to shut down hormone release and stop excessive fluid secretion into the gut. In the third case about a diabetic woman starting a GLP-1 agonist, we discussed how the drug stimulates insulin release only when blood glucose is elevated, which is why it carries a very low risk of hypoglycemia. For the fourth case about a boy with severe obesity and constant hunger from a melanocortin-4 receptor mutation, my friends pointed out that even when his body releases satiety signals, the mutated receptor in his hypothalamus cannot receive the message to feel full.

We then talked through the remaining cases focused on secretions and bile. In the fifth case, a farmer with cholinesterase inhibitor poisoning had excess acetylcholine overstimulating his salivary glands. Because saliva flowed so quickly through the ducts, the ductal cells lacked time to reabsorb sodium and chloride, leaving his saliva more isotonic with higher sodium and bicarbonate levels. The sixth case looked at a man taking high-dose ibuprofen for knee pain. My group mates reviewed how ibuprofen blocks prostaglandin synthesis and weakens the protective gastric mucus barrier, so adding a PPI or misoprostol is necessary to prevent ulcers. In the seventh case, a boy with cystic fibrosis developed recurrent pancreatitis because faulty chloride channels prevented secretin from producing enough watery bicarbonate fluid, causing thick secretions to plug the pancreatic ducts. Lastly, in the primary biliary cholangitis case, my friends explained that activating farnesoid X receptors increases bile flow while stopping new bile acid synthesis, which consequently raises serum cholesterol because less cholesterol is converted into bile acids. Discussing these cases with my friends helped me connect the pre-class lectures to real clinical scenarios.
